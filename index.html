Why I built this

From a research desk to a map anyone can read

As a research associate at the Energy Institute at Haas, I modeled PM2.5 pollution sources and exposure patterns in R and Python for a faculty air-quality policy study. Most of that work lived in notebooks and policy briefs. I wanted a version that answers the everyday question I kept getting from friends: is the air okay right now, and when should I go outside?

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is small enough to reach deep into the lungs and bloodstream, and it’s one of the largest environmental health risks worldwide. Wildfire smoke has made it a Bay Area concern too. Jakarta is on the map because it’s home, and its air is a reminder of how unevenly clean air is distributed.

— Sheina, UC Berkeley Data Science + Haas

Data & method

Where the numbers come from

  • Source: Open-Meteo Air Quality API, built on the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) models. Free, no API key, fetched live in your browser.
  • Modeled, not measured: values are gridded model estimates (CAMS global, roughly a 40 km grid outside Europe), not readings from a sensor on your street. For official alerts, use AirNow.
  • AQI: the US AQI reported by Open-Meteo is the maximum across pollutants, using EPA rolling averages. Colors and health messages follow EPA categories.
  • Model insight: an ordinary least-squares regression of hourly PM2.5 on hour-of-day harmonics plus a linear trend, fit in your browser on one city’s past week. It describes a pattern; it doesn’t forecast.
Related research

Why dust PM2.5 matters on a global map

This explorer is a small, everyday window onto PM2.5. Nearby work in the same Energy Institute orbit goes further on composition: most monitors report total fine particles, but they usually cannot tell smokestack soot from desert dust without specialized sensors.

A research brief by Evan Lingo on the work of Mustafa Zahid sketches a two-stage approach to a global map of dust PM2.5: train a model on low-dust days (location + weather, no dust inputs), then treat the gap between that prediction and actual PM2.5 on dusty days as dust’s contribution, and learn a second model that can estimate dust PM2.5 anywhere from satellite dust and environmental data.

Why it matters: UNICEF has flagged air pollution as a leading global mortality risk; if dust’s share of PM2.5 can be mapped, researchers can study links to health and test whether protections like afforestation (for example China’s Great Green Wall) actually reduce fine-particle dust exposure. I follow this line of work as research context for the Energy Institute air-quality agenda; this live map stays focused on readable hourly PM2.5 / AQI, not dust attribution.
